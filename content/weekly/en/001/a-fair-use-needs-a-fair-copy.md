Answering Joe Patrice, “[DOJ Tells Court AI Training Is Fair Use, Forgets To Mention It’s Negotiating A Stake In OpenAI](https://abovethelaw.com/2026/09/doj-tells-court-ai-training-is-fair-use-forgets-to-mention-its-negotiating-a-stake-in-openai/),” Above the Law, 3 September 2026.

On September 1 the Justice Department told the court in the OpenAI cases that training a model on copyrighted writing is fair use, calling it “[exceedingly transformative](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/09/03/doj-sides-with-openai-warns-obstacles-to-ai-development-threaten-national-security/).” Joe Patrice thinks that conclusion is roughly right and the brief is not: “The brief is bad, though it stumbles toward the correct legal conclusion.” His sharpest point is about what it leaves out, reported talks over a government stake in OpenAI. “It declines to mention the prospects of ownership.”

On that point we agree without reservation. A government arguing for a company it may part-own should say so in the filing.

Where we part company is his proviso. Patrice would call training fair use when the material was legally acquired and the model does not regurgitate it. Most of the dispute sits inside that “legally acquired.” The DOJ itself addressed training only and left acquisition open. The authors’ summary judgment brief, filed September 4 and [described by the Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/plaintiffs-file-motion-for-summary-judgment-v-openai-and-microsoft/), says OpenAI downloaded books from pirate websites and treats market harm as the “single most important element” of fair use. The Anthropic money in this week’s lead exists because a court split those two questions.

“Reading should be fair use,” Patrice writes. It should, for a reader who came by the book honestly. The courts should hold machines to the same test.
