Answering Mark Williams, “[Folk Taxonomy: The Insecurity Behind the Em-Dash Panic](https://thenewpublishingstandard.com/2026/09/13/folk-taxonomy-em-dash-panic/),” The New Publishing Standard, 13 September 2026.

Mark Williams has had enough of the people who claim to spot machine prose by its em dashes and its font. He calls it “a retreat into what linguists call folk taxonomy,” a gut feeling dressed as expertise, and concludes that “the tell was never for one second the em dash or the Aptos font, but coherence itself.” What gets flagged as artificial, he argues, is “overwhelmingly, just edited writing.”

On the dashes he is right, and indie authors should take it to heart. Stripping punctuation from a manuscript to look human is editing for a suspicious stranger instead of the reader. A good sentence should not have to apologise for being well made.

We would put more weight on a point he makes almost in passing. Williams grants that some questions are real, disclosure among them, and he names the non-native writers whom detection tools single out because their prose is “too clean.” This week shows those questions arriving at the book trade. Spotify labels its digitally narrated audiobooks. OUP now expects book authors to disclose significant AI use, and found most researchers unsure how.

That is the better answer to the dash hunters. Not a scrubbed manuscript, but a plain account of which tools you used, kept from the first draft and offered where a retailer or publisher asks. Folk taxonomy thrives on guesswork. A record leaves it nothing to guess.
